9. Thanks to an undersea fiber-optic cable connection to Europe and broadband satellite services, Greenland is well connected, with 93% of its population having internet access. Your hotel or host, if you are staying in a guesthouse, will probably have Wi-Fi or an internet-connected computer, and all settlements have internet cafés or public Wi-Fi locations. As mentioned above, the term "Eskimo" is acceptable in the United States, but many non-Americans, particularly in Canada, consider it offensive. Although you may hear native Greenlanders using the term, foreigners should avoid it. Indigenous Greenlanders refer to themselves as Inuit in Canada and Greenlanders or Kalaallit (singular: Kalaaleq) in Greenland. Crime and unfair treatment of foreigners are practically unknown in Greenland. Even in towns, there are no "bad neighborhoods." The cold weather is probably the only real danger an unprepared visitor may face. If you plan to visit Greenland during winter, remember that temperatures become colder the farther north you travel, so you must bring plenty of warm clothing. The long summer days can cause excessive sleepiness and related health problems. During summer, beware of Arctic mosquitoes. Although they do not transmit diseases, their bites can be irritating.

16. Keep an eye on your bags when other people are handling your luggage, especially when crossing international borders. You may discover that you have unknowingly been turned into a drug courier, which could get you into serious trouble. This also applies while waiting in line, as drug-sniffing dogs may be deployed without warning at any time. 20. For much of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, it was believed that New Zealand's first inhabitants were the Māori, who hunted large birds called moas. A later theory proposed that the Māori migrated from Polynesia in a great fleet and took New Zealand from the Moriori to establish an agricultural society. However, new evidence suggests that the Moriori were a group of mainland Māori who migrated from New Zealand to the Chatham Islands, where they developed their own distinctive, peaceful culture. Another Māori tribe also settled on the Chatham Islands after migrating from New Zealand. They called themselves Moriori, and several conflicts occurred between the groups, eventually leading to the destruction of the Moriori population.
